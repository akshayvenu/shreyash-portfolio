Offboarding Portal

The five-step exit of one employee, designed so that money, property and goodwill all land correctly — and so the last thing the company does to someone isn't sloppy.

sec 02
The problem

Onboarding is a flow everyone designs. Offboarding is the one everyone leaves as a mail thread — and it is the higher-stakes half. Money moves, company property is out in the world, and someone is having one of the worst weeks of their working life while an HR manager chases four departments for sign-off.

The structural difficulty is that the middle of the flow isn't sequential. Accounts, IT, Admin and the Project Manager clear in parallel, on their own timelines, and the settlement cannot be computed until all four are done. A linear wizard models that badly; a free-form checklist loses the gate.

So the design problem was a sequential spine with one parallel stage inside it — and a tone that stays procedural without being cold.

sec 03
The structure

Five stages, one of them parallel

A single case moves through five stages — Initiation, Clearance, Settlement, Dispatch, Completed. The stepper carrying them sits in the same position with the same three states (done, active, upcoming) on every screen that handles a case, so it doubles as the honest answer to “how far along is this”.

The stages are not the same thing as the screens. The portal has five of those too, but the first is a Dashboard — a queue of every open case, with no stepper on it, because it belongs to no single case. Dispatch has no screen of its own: the four exit documents are generated inside the settlement screen once the payout is approved.

1 done
Initiation

Exit type, last day, notice, handover-to. One form, and the only place a human types.

2 parallel
Clearance

Four departments, 15 items, independent timelines. The stage that isn't a step.

3 gated

4 upcoming

Four documents from templates, variables highlighted for review. Lives inside the settlement screen, not its own.

5 terminal
Completed

The completion audit log — eight events, no actions. A closed case with no button to press.

sec 04
The decision that made it work

A department is cleared or it isn't

There is no partial credit. Progress reads 3 of 4 departments, not 13 of 15 items — because “87% cleared” tells an HR manager nothing actionable, while “Accounts is not cleared” tells them exactly who to call. Each department carries its own colour, so a glance down the tab bar identifies the blocker without reading a word.

This is the state the tracker opens in — one department short, and the case study says so rather than showing a tidier finished grid. Accounts clears on May 5 in the audit log, which is when the settlement unlocks.

Clearing needs a name attached

Marking an item cleared opens a modal that requires a date and a person — the confirm button stays disabled until “cleared by” has a value. An unattributed clearance is how a laptop goes missing with nobody responsible.

The date warns, it doesn't block

A clearance dated after the last working day shows a warning but still submits. Late clearances are real; refusing to record them just means someone backdates instead.

Settlement is genuinely locked

Step 3 does not open until all four departments are cleared. The gate is the whole reason the parallel stage can stay parallel without losing control.

sec 06
The screen I'd defend hardest

Show the arithmetic

A final settlement that shows only totals is a document someone will dispute. Every line carries its calculation in a middle column — 31 days × ₹5,000, 12 days encashed, 4y 2m of gratuity — so the person receiving it can check the maths themselves. Zero rows stay visible and greyed rather than being hidden, because an absent line reads as an oversight.

Then it makes you type CONFIRM

Approving the settlement requires typing the word CONFIRM. It is deliberately annoying: this action pays out ₹2.79 lakh and terminates an employment record, and it should not be one misplaced click away from a dispute.

The four exit documents work the same way — generated from templates with every substituted variable visually highlighted, so an HR manager reviews the values rather than skimming a finished-looking letter.

sec 07
Decisions worth defending

Four calls, and what each cost

DecisionReasoning · trade-off
Progress counts departments, not items“Accounts is not cleared” is actionable; 13 of 15 is trivia. Costs the satisfying incremental progress bar — an HR manager clearing two items sees no movement until the department completes.
Every clearance needs a named personConfirm stays disabled until “cleared by” is filled. Costs three seconds per item across 15 items — and buys someone to ask when the laptop isn't in the cupboard.
Type CONFIRM to approve the payoutFriction proportional to consequence. Costs goodwill with anyone processing exits in volume, which is the right group to inconvenience for this particular action.
Step 5 has no actions at allA closed case is a record, not a workspace — only Download and Close. Costs the ability to correct a mistake in place; a correction has to reopen the case, which is the honest behaviour anyway.
What I'd change

The whole flow is designed for the HR manager. The person actually leaving never sees a screen — they receive four documents by email and otherwise experience this as silence. A candidate-facing view showing where their clearance and settlement stand would cost little and remove most of the anxiety of an exit. I didn't build it because nobody asked, which isn't a good reason.

What it proved

Friction is a design material. Almost every instinct in product design says remove steps — but a required name, a typed CONFIRM and a screen with no buttons are all deliberate friction, each placed where the cost of being wrong is highest. Knowing where to add resistance is the same skill as knowing where to remove it.
