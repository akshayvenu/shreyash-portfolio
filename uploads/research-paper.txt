Emotionally Adaptive UI/UX for Menstrual Cycle Tracking: A Longitudinal, Mixed-Methods Study of Phase-Linked Affective Variation and Its Design Implications
Shreyash Patekar
MCA, KJ SOMAIYA OF MANAGEMENT, Mumbai
Abstract—Most menstruation tracking apps treat the cycle as a data entry challenge, which dates entered and forecasts generated. This process is one of the few repeating human experiences that continuously alters mood, vitality, and self-perception, although it is rarely treated as an emotional one. This research investigates the variations in women's emotional states during the menstrual cycle, as well as the implications for interfaces built to support, rather than log. Ten active participants (aged 18 to 35) conducted 22 check-ins over a 30-day period using organised Google Forms, ranking their emotional status, physical comfort, energy, and mental attention on a 1-10 scale with open-ended questions. Quantitative research revealed a continuous affective spectrum, with a follicular-period well-being high, a monthly trough marked by a 68% energy decline, and a luteal. phase that was not consistently difficult, but bimodal, with people alternating between calm and anguish. Thematic coding revealed phase-specific emotional fingerprints as well as other patterns that departed from the medical stereotype, most notably the fact that life stress consistently outweighed hormonal phase and subjective &quot;brain fog&quot; was accompanied by reasonably preserved self-rated attention. The behavioural analysis was similarly revealing: retention dropped 83% before the last check-in, rapid responders outperformed detailed responders, and 80 percent refused to answer libido-related questions. More than half of the group showed signs of melancholy and did not communicate with their spouses about the cycle. We incorporate these results within an emotional responsive design framework and a suggested application, NurtureSync, in which interface language, micro-interactions, check-in time, and support information are tailored based on the client's current phase and emotions load. This paper is not a declaration that empathy-aware design is novel in the broader sense, but rather a grounded description of what emotional requirements exist, how they vary, and the manner in which an interface might address them without delving into clinical terrain.
Index terms include affective designing, emotive computing, menstruation observation, UX investigation, private informatics, long-term studies, mood, adaptable interfaces, FemTech, and mental health. 

I. Introduction
In general, digital devices can forecast many biological processes that occur in our bodies. A menstrual cycle tracker can accurately estimate when a person will have their period. However, it will not anticipate the user's emotional state during their cycle. This research was motivated by an unmet design need.
Currently, there are numerous menstrual cycle monitoring applications on the market with a variety of capabilities. Most are large-scale calendars that keep track of when users started, when they are expected to be fertile, when they are expected to finish bleeding, and any symptoms entered by the user. As product developers, we presume that everyone is addressing their company's concerns. One especially intriguing aspect of menstrual cycle tracking is that almost everyone finds it difficult to relate to emotionally. Menstruation is largely a physical event, but it is also associated with a wide range of emotional sensations. As a result, developing a product that only displays a calendar and gives information about it will not provide consumers with the emotional support they require when they are on their period.
Designing a product based on the user's emotional experience is not a new concept. Donald Norman recognised three types of emotional responses when utilising a product: visceral, behavioural, and introspective.There are numerous HCI applications [1] that describe how emotion plays a role in interaction and creates unique emotional experiences. They contend that emotion is an essential component of product functionality, rather than just a way to adorn a product with extra features. There is relatively little study on how these theories are used in a cyclical, emotionalised domain, however the menstrual cycle has been recognised as an acceptable setting for an experimental interface test due to its emotional predictability.The study began with a design probe investigating the development of emotionally intelligent interfaces, but as more information about the predictability of the menstrual cycle became available, the focus shifted to the menstrual tracking domain, which provided a repeatable standard emotional model that could be developed into a robust response mechanism. Rather than making assumptions during the development process, the project began with primary data obtained from a longitudinal study of how real women reported their cycles in their native language during the previous several weeks. The study's key research aims included learning:RQ1: How do women's physical comfort, emotional mood, energy level, and mental clarity change during the phases of their menstrual cycle, and do those changes vary greatly amongst individuals?RQ2: How do women characterise their emotions and suffering during the four stages of their menstrual cycle, and how do such descriptions differ from the clinical mental image of the menstrual cycle?RQ3: What do women track as part of their menstrual cycle, what do they not track, when do they cease tracking their menstrual cycle, and what evidence does the data provide concerning friction in current tools?RQ4: How may empirical data be leveraged to make concrete interface design decisions for a menstrual cycle tracker with emotional adaptability?The paper will be organised as follows: a description of relevant literature, methodology, themed sub-sections for expanded presentation of the findings, theme-based discussion, and a design section that connects the qualitative themes and findings to the design of the interface elements within the proposed application. We have taken care to describe the research sample and provide explicit descriptions of the level of trust in the data for drawing final findings and/or suggestions.

This paper presents the study's context within the framework of three research areas: affective and expressive planning, emotional neurology of the menstrual cycle, and the private informatics literature on monitoring and disengagement. A. Emotion and Affective Design.
According to Norman's definition of visceral, behavioural, and cognitive creation/processing [1], usefulness is defined by both feel and functionality; thus, the ways a thing feels first (directly) and over time (reflectively), combined with its enjoyment level, ultimately determines whether the product is usable. In that sense, it is correct to say that the way that a human works with an emotionally intelligent device will be more logical than conversing with an insane computer; this is an area Picard researched, with sympathetic computing being one of the outcomes [2]. Later research in &quot;Human Computer Interaction&quot; identified emotion as an essential part of interaction between humans and computers [3]. People, such as Walter,, have written concerning their interactions with emotional objects, providing real-world instances of how a psychological attraction with an object,, through personality traits and resonance, can result in feelings of belonging to that item, as in Walter's example [4]. Other writings by Hassenzahl distinguishes between pragmatic and hedonic development [5]; a gadget may work, but if it does not produce a hedonic profit from a the investment made, the client is unlikely to desire return. Höök defines affective circuits [6] as a mutually generated emotional connection between the user and the system through repetitive interaction, which is especially relevant for a tool that is used multiple times.

C.  Tracking, Personal Informatics, and Disengagement
The published material on individual informatics takes a different view on self-tracking than many people believe. Tracking is viewed through the lens of a stage model, because proposed by Li, Dey, and Forlizzi, as a process that occurs in multiple stages: preparation, disposal, integration, standardisation, and execution. At any of the above stages, the user has the option to stop going any further. According to Epstein (2003) and others, people frequently abandon and begin tracking, and a significant amount of technology fails at the action-to-idea stage, where data collected by tracking transforms into meaning. The menstrual watching study carried out by Li, Dey, and Forlizzi focused on how women's necessity of tracking goes far beyond predicting when their period will occur. These women desired to grasp the significance and context of their bodies, but the technology available did not always meet their expectations. A similar acknowledgement of the challenges associated with the use of sensitive data for estimating birthrates and developing cycle technologies supports many users' long-standing concerns about the delicate nature of the information captured by these programs and the effects of such information on their lives. The more typical definitions such as usability and convincing design failed to account for the goal of applying the definitions in the daily use of highly emotional, privacy sensitive products. 
D.  Research Gap
There were three gaps observed. First, while designing to feed emotions is widely discussed in the abstract, it is rarely applied to a real-world cyclical environment (an emotional context that changes on a regular basis), so there may be a connection that can adapt to this periodic change. Second, while there is data in the literature supporting the idea that each stage of a cycle have real effects, these outcomes vary so that when organisations design &quot;trackers&quot; for observing people, they are prone to use only one kind of user, despite the fact that numerous users exist. Third, while the literature on private computing provides insights into why people cease using gadgets or trackers, it fails to account for the unique emotional et public privacy constraints associated with documenting menstruation. The study attempts to fill all three gaps by using longitudinal data of how women responded with cycle recording and how phase tracking enabled a more emotional responsive design.. 

III.  METHODOLOGY
Applying a Permanent Mixed Technique Design to Identify a Dynamic Variable. Periodic hybrid studies are intended to investigate the growth of a thing over a set period of time. Because enquiries are not an ideal instrument for identifying a cycles (because patterns only arise over many consecutive weeks), the investigators utilised repetition among all the respondents; thus, the duration of the collection was cut into five phases of time alongside the intention to ensure that each milestone would place a person in a different stages of development stage (i.e., cycle), and conducted a combination of quantitative analyses along with individual reflections to establish a space for data to be addressed. 
A.  Instrument Design
For gathering data, a standardised Google form was used, which included one screening questions and four check-in sheets. The screening questionnaire collected information on participants' gender band, calendar regularity, average period length, current phase day, and any recent menstrual issues they had. At each check-in, participants rated four metrics (&quot;emotional state, physical relaxation, energy level, as well as mental focus) on a scale of 1 to 10. Participants also checked off items on a checklist of symptoms of illness, mental states, changes in mood, sleep routines, and social interest. Finally, participants were asked to complete open-ended questions about how they felt and why. A few issues, including libido, were taken into account to examine potential blind spots of the monitoring mechanism; however, these did not yield useful data as each participant chose not to respond to this question, leaving little to no insight about their responses. 
This gadget was created to assist the user in identifying their menstrual cycle while keeping a record of the date it is and asking consumers directly on an application what cycle day it is. This resulted in inconsistencies among the people surveyed who used this product. Testing the app's results revealed who had a healthy or regular cycle and allowed them to explain the significance of their findings. 
B.  Participant Selection and Screening
Every one of the participants volunteered and received no financial reward for their time. Participants' emotions were expected to vary depending on their age group, so they were divided into four age groups: teens (10 to 17), younger people (18-25), adults (26-35), and older adults (35+, including pre-menopause). This division was necessary for lumping any of these women into a single group would have obscured the difference on emotional responses when compare aged women to old men. The final sample consisted primarily of women aged 18 to 35, showing a clustering between 18 and 25. Furthermore, a few of the women had consistent menstrual cycles, whereas others had unpredictable cycles. D.  Emotional Mapping and Analytical Approach
The undertaking was evaluated using two independent techniques: qualitative and quantitative methods. The quantitative evaluation focused on the formulation of descriptive stats for ratings on a 0 to 10 scale to summarise the data that was collected on a per phases base (as well as per person basis) and to take into account these evaluations on a per-phase along with participant basis (a single per participant was added if they had no less than three check-ins). Although the sample size of respondents was insufficient to conduct implicit statistical tests, ratings provided were taken as descriptive or signal-based, rather than proof of anything. Qualitative examinations of open-ended responses via subjects were also organised according to theme by phase, encompassing the following items: emotional parts that appeared frequently throughout the phases, certain word choices/rates adopted by subjects, painful areas identified by subjects, and unfulfilled needs expressed or proposed by subjects. The data show that women's standard of life varies depending on her cycles. A woman's quality of lifestyle is highest throughout a follicular stage of her development in three areas: emotion, physical comfort, and mental focus, and there is little variation between other women in the identical phase as her. When women's quality of life is at its pinnacle, they will achieve nearly equal degree of success. In contrast, a woman's level of life is at a minimum during her menstrual period, with a substantial decline in quality scores over her affective &amp; energy levels.

B.  The Emotional Texture Behind the Numbers
The ratings show the cycle's structure, while the open-ended responses describe what it's like to live in it. We retrieved four phase fingerprints. 
Menstruation read as overwhelm and depletion.  Menstruation was associated with feelings of overwhelm, fatigue, and, most significantly, a debilitating sense of tiredness. Weariness was usually the dominant feeling, yet it developed into physical pain because fatigue and discomfort were frequently experienced concurrently. One participant said, &quot;I feel happy at first and afterwards before a period I am really disinterested and then I think nothing and I feel every bit of that.&quot; Another said, &quot;I have awful abdominal tenderness on day one, and it takes me throughout the entirety of the day to find the opportunity to try and relax, but being connected to my family means that I'm always currently doing anything at that point and therefore it can be a stressful day for me.&quot; 83% of participants who reported cramping during this time period said it had no emotional impact on them. .
